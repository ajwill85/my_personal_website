## The Three-Legged Stool of Trust
![Three-Legged Stool](/blog/stool-white.png)

Just as the cybersecurity world descended upon RSAC in San Francisco recently, major allegations dropped regarding a GRC platform, Delve. An [anonymous poster](https://substack.com/home/post/p-191342187) created a substack detailing the allegations, accusing Delve of being in cahoots with auditors who helped rubberstamp SOC2 audit reports for their clients. Delve has since denied these allegations but the damage to their reputation, as well as the audit firms involved, has been done. One thing which stands out to me is the lack of attention given to the users of Delve, the customers who were complicit in the scheme. 

## No Leg to Stand On

In the process of certifying any company against SOC2, ISO 27001, or any other framework, there are at least two, and often times three parties involved. The company being certified, the auditor performing the audit, and a third party which may be an internal auditor or a consultant hired to help the company prepare for the audit. Typically a software provider is not involved in the audit process at all, other than using the software provider's GRC platform to manage their evidence. In the case of Delve, according to the annoymous poster, Delve was involved in the audit process by providing customers with pre-written audit reports that their customers could quickly submit to recommended auditors for quick approval.

This requires at least two parties to be in cahoots, in this case Delve and at least one audit firm. However, it does not absolve the clients of their part in the process...submitting false or pre-written evidence to auditors. In order for this scheme to work the software platform would need to provide templates (pre-written evidence), customers would need to submit the templates as evidence with minimal edits, and auditors would need to accept the evidence without proper review. This means that the customers are complicit in the scheme, and are just as guilty as the software platform and the auditors, if the allegations are true. 

Typically we would not look to the provider of the templates, whether a GRC platform or a consultant, as being complicit in the scheme. Generally we view the customer as being responisble for the evidence they present to the auditor, and expect the auditor to perform due diligence and ensure that the evidence is accurate and complete. Unfortunately it seems in this case, based on video evidence provided by the anonymous poster as well as copies of over 400 Delve client documents, that Delve was aware of at least one of the auditors accepting clients information in order to rubberstamp their SOC2 audit reports and provide a fast 1-2 week process vs the typical 6-12 month certification process. They claimed their automation of the process to collect evidence is what led to efficiency in completing the audit in such a short timeframe, but it appears that this is not the full story. 

## Collateral Damage

The real victim here is not Delve, not the auditors involved, and maybe not even the customers of Delve. The real victims are the third parties who trusted that Delve's customers certifications were legitimate, and may have even entered into contracts with them based on those certifications. [Duped by Delve](https://dupedbydelve.com/) is a website created to expose the Delve SOC2 scandal and provide resources for those who have been affected by it. There's a list of the auditors, customers, and 3rd parties who may have been affected given their relationship with Delve's customers. 

It is also a black eye for the cybersecurity industry, and a lost of trust in the current certification process. Certifications are an important factor when doing due diligence on a business, and not having SOC 2 or other certifications can be a deal breaker for many companies. As a startup, the faster you can get SOC 2 or other certifications, the faster you can start doing business with larger companies. 

So when you look at the Delve situation, don't forget about the customers who were complicit in the scheme. Many are likely just as guilty as Delve and the auditors involved. Without their acceptance and submission of pre-written evidence, this scheme would not have been possible. Is there a [pattern](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/04/embattled-startup-delve-has-parted-ways-with-y-combinator/) in the customers involved? Is there a deeper cultural concern?

A three-legged stool cannot stand if a single leg is compromised. In this case, at least 2, if not all three legs of the stool were compromised, and the entire system is at risk of collapse.
